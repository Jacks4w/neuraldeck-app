Lesson 1 - Oscillators and Waveforms

Okay everyone, welcome to the first lesson of the synthesizer course. Today we start from the very beginning of the signal chain: the oscillator. In a classic subtractive synthesizer the oscillator is the sound source. Everything else, the filter, the amplifier, the envelopes, only shapes what the oscillator produces. So if you understand oscillators, you understand where every sound starts.

An oscillator is a circuit, or a piece of code, that produces a periodic signal, a signal that repeats itself over and over. The number of repetitions per second is the frequency, measured in hertz. If the waveform repeats 440 times per second, we hear the note A above middle C, which we call A4. Double the frequency to 880 hertz and you get one octave higher. Halve it to 220 hertz and you are one octave lower. Remember this: one octave equals a doubling or halving of frequency.

On analog synthesizers like the Minimoog or the Prophet-5 the oscillator is called a VCO, a voltage controlled oscillator. The pitch is controlled by a voltage. Most classic instruments follow the one volt per octave standard: if you raise the control voltage by one volt, the pitch goes up exactly one octave. Digital synthesizers use DCOs, digitally controlled oscillators, or completely digital oscillators, which are much more stable in tuning. That is why old VCO synths need to warm up and drift a little, and many people actually love that slight instability because it makes the sound feel alive.

Now let's talk about the four classic waveforms. The first is the sine wave. The sine wave is the purest sound: it contains only the fundamental frequency, no harmonics at all. It sounds soft, round, a bit like a flute or a whistle. Because it has no harmonics, a filter has almost nothing to remove, so the sine wave is not very interesting for subtractive synthesis, but it is the building block of additive and FM synthesis.

The second waveform is the triangle wave. The triangle contains only odd harmonics, the third, the fifth, the seventh and so on, and they decrease in level very quickly, with the square of the harmonic number. So the triangle sounds a bit brighter than a sine, but still mellow. Think of it as a sine with a little bit of edge.

The third is the sawtooth wave, sometimes called the ramp. The sawtooth contains all harmonics, both even and odd, and their amplitude decreases as one over the harmonic number. This makes the sawtooth the brightest and buzziest of the classic waves. It is perfect for brass sounds, strings, and those huge supersaw leads you hear in trance music. If you are in doubt, start your patch with a sawtooth: it gives the filter a lot of material to work with.

The fourth is the square wave. Like the triangle, the square wave contains only odd harmonics, but they decrease more slowly, as one over the harmonic number. The result is a hollow, woody sound, a bit like a clarinet. The square wave is a special case of the pulse wave. A pulse wave has a parameter called pulse width, or duty cycle: the percentage of time the wave stays in the high position. At fifty percent you get a perfect square. As you move the pulse width toward ten percent or ninety percent the sound gets thinner and more nasal. When you modulate the pulse width slowly with an LFO you get pulse width modulation, PWM, which creates that rich, chorus-like movement typical of eighties string pads. We will talk about LFOs in lesson three.

A very important concept is the harmonic series. Almost every musical sound is made of a fundamental plus a series of harmonics, which are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. If the fundamental is 100 hertz, the harmonics are at 200, 300, 400 hertz and so on. The balance between these harmonics is what we call timbre. That is why a violin and a trumpet playing the same note sound different: same fundamental, different harmonic recipe.

Most synthesizers have more than one oscillator. With two or three oscillators you can detune them slightly, for example by five or ten cents, and the small difference in frequency creates beating, a slow periodic variation that makes the sound thicker and wider. You can also tune one oscillator an octave or a fifth above the other to create richer chords from a single key. Another classic technique is oscillator sync, or hard sync: the slave oscillator is forced to restart its cycle every time the master oscillator starts a new cycle. If you sweep the pitch of the slave oscillator you get that aggressive, tearing lead sound you can hear in many eighties records.

Finally, many synths also include a noise generator. White noise contains all frequencies at equal energy per hertz, and it sounds like a hiss. Pink noise has equal energy per octave, so it sounds darker and more natural. Noise is essential for percussion, wind effects, ocean waves and for adding breath to a sound.

Homework for next week: take any synthesizer, even a free plugin, use a single oscillator with the filter fully open, and listen carefully to the difference between sine, triangle, sawtooth and square at the same pitch. Then try two detuned sawtooth oscillators. Next lesson we will see how the filter sculpts these waveforms.
