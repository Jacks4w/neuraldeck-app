Lesson 2 - Filters, Cutoff and Resonance

Welcome back. Last time we generated raw waveforms with the oscillators. Today we meet the most characteristic module of subtractive synthesis: the filter. The idea of subtractive synthesis is simple. You start with a harmonically rich waveform, like a sawtooth, and you remove, you subtract, the frequencies you don't want. The filter is the tool that does the subtraction.

On analog synths the filter is called a VCF, a voltage controlled filter, because its main parameter can be controlled by a voltage, for example by an envelope or an LFO.

The most important parameter of any filter is the cutoff frequency. The cutoff is the point where the filter starts to attenuate the signal. Let's start with the most common type, the low-pass filter. A low-pass filter lets the frequencies below the cutoff pass and attenuates the frequencies above it. If you close the cutoff, moving it down, the sound becomes darker and more muffled. If you open the cutoff the sound becomes brighter, until with the cutoff fully open you hear the raw oscillator. The classic filter sweep, slowly opening a low-pass filter on a sawtooth, is probably the most famous sound in electronic music.

The opposite is the high-pass filter. It removes frequencies below the cutoff and lets the high frequencies pass. It is great to thin out a sound, to remove low-end mud from pads so they don't fight with the bass, or to create airy, thin textures. Then we have the band-pass filter, which lets only a band of frequencies around the cutoff pass and removes both lows and highs. Band-pass sounds are nasal, telephone-like, and very useful for vocal-like effects. Finally the notch filter, or band-reject, removes a narrow band around the cutoff and keeps everything else. It creates a subtle hollow effect, and when you sweep it you get something similar to a phaser.

The second key concept is the slope of the filter, also called the order or the number of poles. The slope tells you how steeply the filter attenuates frequencies beyond the cutoff. It is measured in decibels per octave. Each pole adds 6 dB per octave. So a one-pole filter has a gentle 6 dB per octave slope, a two-pole filter has 12 dB per octave, and a four-pole filter has 24 dB per octave. The legendary Moog ladder filter is a four-pole, 24 dB per octave low-pass, with a fat and warm character. The Oberheim SEM filter is a two-pole, 12 dB per octave state variable filter, softer and more open, and it can morph between low-pass, notch and high-pass. The Roland TB-303 uses a filter that is usually described as roughly 18 dB per octave, and that slightly unusual slope is part of the acid sound.

The third parameter is resonance, also called emphasis or Q. Resonance boosts the frequencies right around the cutoff point by feeding part of the filter output back into its input. With low resonance the filter is smooth. As you increase resonance you get a peak at the cutoff frequency, and the sound becomes more nasal, squelchy and vocal. When you sweep the cutoff with high resonance you hear that whistling, wah-like effect. If you push resonance to the maximum on many analog filters, the filter starts self-oscillating: it produces a pure sine wave at the cutoff frequency even with no input. Many synthesists use a self-oscillating filter as an extra sine oscillator, and if the filter has keyboard tracking you can even play it in tune.

Keyboard tracking, also called key follow, is the amount by which the cutoff follows the note you play. Without key tracking, the cutoff stays fixed, so high notes sound darker relative to low notes because more of their harmonics fall above the cutoff. With one hundred percent key tracking the cutoff moves exactly with the pitch, so the timbre stays consistent across the keyboard.

Remember also that resonance on many filters, especially the Moog ladder, reduces the bass. When you turn up resonance the low end gets thinner. Some modern filters have a compensation circuit for this. This is one reason why acid basslines sound thin and squelchy instead of deep.

In practice, the cutoff is almost never static. We modulate it. The most common source is an envelope: a quick envelope that opens the filter at the start of each note and then closes it gives you a plucky, percussive sound. The amount of envelope applied to the cutoff is called envelope amount or filter envelope depth. We will see envelopes in detail next time, together with LFOs.
