Lesson 3 - Envelopes, LFOs and Modulation

Hi everyone. In the first two lessons we built the static part of the patch: oscillators make the sound, the filter shapes the spectrum. But a sound that never changes over time is boring. Today we bring the patch to life with modulation, and specifically with the two most important modulation sources: envelopes and LFOs.

Let's start with the envelope generator, often abbreviated EG. An envelope is a control signal that is triggered when you press a key and that describes how a parameter changes over time during the note. The most common type is the ADSR envelope, which has four stages: Attack, Decay, Sustain and Release.

Attack is the time it takes for the envelope to go from zero to its maximum level after you press the key. A short attack, a few milliseconds, gives you a percussive start, like a piano or a pluck. A long attack, one or two seconds, gives you a slow fade-in, typical of pads and bowed strings.

Decay is the time it takes to fall from the maximum level down to the sustain level.

Sustain is different from the other three stages: it is not a time, it is a level. It is the level the envelope holds as long as you keep the key pressed. If sustain is at zero, the sound dies away during the decay even if you keep holding the key, which is perfect for plucks and percussive sounds. If sustain is at maximum, the decay has no effect and the sound stays at full level, like an organ.

Release is the time it takes for the envelope to go from the sustain level back to zero after you release the key. A short release cuts the sound immediately; a long release lets it ring out, like a reverb tail.

Typically a synth has at least two envelopes. The amplitude envelope controls the VCA, the voltage controlled amplifier, so it shapes the volume of the note. The filter envelope controls the cutoff of the filter, so it shapes the brightness over time. A classic bass patch: amplitude envelope with fast attack, medium decay, high sustain and short release; filter envelope with fast attack, short decay and low sustain, so every note starts bright and quickly becomes darker. That gives the famous plucky bass sound.

Some synths offer simpler envelopes, like AD or AR, and some offer more complex ones, with extra stages like hold or delay, or multi-segment envelopes that you can draw freely. Envelopes can also have velocity sensitivity: the harder you hit the key, the higher the envelope peak.

Now the LFO, the low frequency oscillator. An LFO is an oscillator, exactly like the ones we saw in lesson one, but it runs at very low frequencies, usually between about 0.1 hertz and 20 hertz, below the range of human hearing. We don't listen to the LFO directly: we use it to modulate other parameters in a cyclic, repeating way. The LFO typically offers the same waveforms as an audio oscillator, sine, triangle, sawtooth, square, plus random waveforms like sample and hold, which produces random steps.

The three classic LFO destinations are pitch, amplitude and filter cutoff. When the LFO modulates pitch you get vibrato. Keep the depth small and the rate around five or six hertz for a natural vibrato. When the LFO modulates amplitude you get tremolo, a cyclic change in volume. When the LFO modulates the filter cutoff you get a wah or wobble effect: a sine or triangle LFO on a low-pass filter is the basis of the dubstep wobble bass, especially when the LFO rate is synced to the tempo of the song, for example eighth notes or sixteenth notes. And as we said in lesson one, an LFO on pulse width gives you PWM.

Important LFO parameters are rate, the speed; depth or amount, how much the LFO affects the destination; waveform; and sometimes delay or fade-in, which lets the LFO start gently after the note begins, very useful for realistic vibrato. Many LFOs also have a key sync or retrigger option, which restarts the LFO cycle with each new note, versus free running, where the LFO keeps going regardless of the keyboard.

All of this is organized in the modulation matrix. A mod matrix is a table where you connect sources, like envelopes, LFOs, velocity, aftertouch or the mod wheel, to destinations, like pitch, cutoff, resonance or pan, and set an amount for each connection. On modular synthesizers the mod matrix is physical: you connect sources and destinations with patch cables. That freedom is why modular synthesis is so powerful, and also why it can be confusing at the beginning.

To summarize the whole first module of the course: the oscillator generates the sound, the filter shapes the timbre, the amplifier controls the volume, and envelopes and LFOs move all of these parameters over time.
